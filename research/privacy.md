## Candidate P1 — Audit-Budget Sensitivity of Privacy–Utility Evaluation for Synthetic Tabular Data

**Recommendation:** good top-three candidate when a privacy project is preferred. Easy–Moderate implementation; moderate interpretation. Strong reproduction path, modest originality. Core contribution is a controlled measurement/reproducibility study, not a new anonymization method.

### Problem, relevance, and literature

The research question is whether a small, affordable privacy audit gives the same conclusions as a larger audit, and how uncertainty or failed attacks change a comparison of synthetic-data releases. A privacy claim based on a single point estimate can be misleading if the attack sample is small or ineffective. The project studies this empirically with public tabular data, without collecting sensitive data or proving differential privacy.

### What the base paper actually does

Anonymeter treats the generator as a black box and audits the released table. Its attacks try to isolate a record, link partial records, or infer a withheld attribute. It compares attack success on the generator's training records with success on a separate control set from the same population, and with a naive baseline. This distinguishes learnable population patterns from excess information about training records. It reports risk with confidence intervals, and requires an effective main attack relative to the naive baseline.

The paper uses Adult, a 1940 US Census sample, and Texas hospital discharge data. Adult has 39,074 training and 9,768 control rows. The other datasets use 50,000 train plus 50,000 control rows, after selecting 37 and 28 columns. It tests a deliberately leaky release, CTGAN and DPCTGAN. Its controlled leakage experiment uses 2,000 attacks and varies leaked-record fraction and auxiliary information. The generator evaluation measures distributional/query fidelity as a composite utility score, not downstream predictive F1.

Table 3 reports for Adult CTGAN: singling-out risk 0.0548 ± 0.0134; linkability 0.0012 ± 0.0003; inference 0.0161 ± 0.0031; utility 88/100. Adult DPCTGAN has lower risks on these three reported means and utility 79. These are aggregates over many attack settings; **a single example-notebook result is not directly comparable to Table 3**. The controlled experiment finds risk increasing with inserted leakage; linkability is generally smaller than the other risks. The paper's privacy and utility metrics must not be equated with classification accuracy or DP epsilon.

### Limitations: distinguish evidence from proposed contribution

**Author-stated:** Section 4.3 says empirical attacks provide a lower bound on leakage, dependent on attack strength. The main attack must beat the naive baseline to support interpretation. Section 4.1 assumes independent records, each corresponding to an individual, with a separate same-population control set. The experiments mainly report dataset-wide risks. Section 7 suggests stronger attacks, subgroup/individual analysis and fairness extensions. The example notebook warns about failures to find enough singling-out predicates and explains the runtime/precision trade-off in attack count.

**Critical saturation finding:** The **current upstream repository already provides arbitrary inference predictors and group-wise secret-column risk estimation**, including `attribute_inference_custom_model_and_group_wise_risk.ipynb`. Therefore “add random forest attacks,” “add subgroup auditing,” and “add confidence intervals” are not credible new methods. The base paper also already varies auxiliary-attribute counts. Sources: [current README](https://github.com/statice/anonymeter), [example notebook](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/statice/anonymeter/main/notebooks/anonymeter_example.ipynb).

**Our identified extension:** measure, under a predeclared small compute budget, the repeatability of conclusions across attack budgets and seeds, including failed/ineffective attacks instead of silently reporting only successful runs. Ask whether a cheap audit changes the direction or certainty of generator comparisons, and quantify the smallest budget sufficient for the chosen experimental resolution. This is a modest empirical extension; a literature search cannot establish worldwide novelty. It should be presented as a reproducibility and evaluation study, not a novel privacy guarantee. Its value is a reproducible budget–uncertainty–runtime curve and an account of what affordable audits can and cannot conclude.

### Exact contribution and bounded implementation

1. Reproduce all three Anonymeter attack types against the **already-generated Adult CTGAN release** in the official repository. This avoids spending the first two weeks training a GAN.
2. Add a Gaussian-copula synthetic release fitted to the **same supplied original training table**, and generate the same number of records. Use [SDV GaussianCopulaSynthesizer](https://docs.sdv.dev/sdv/modeling/single-table-synthesizers/gaussiancopulasynthesizer), which is CPU-friendly. Pin versions; document that this additional generator is our comparison rather than an exact repeat of the base paper's generator experiment.
3. Fix a small, declared attack configuration for each attack type. For example, four-column multivariate singling out, a fixed two-plus-two split of auxiliary features for linkability, and income inference from a declared set of demographic/employment features. Keep settings unchanged across budgets and generators.
4. Compare 250, 1,000, and 2,000 attack targets, with five independent audit repetitions. Two releases × three budgets × three attack types × five repeats = 90 audit runs. Run a pilot first and reduce repetitions to three if necessary; do not enlarge the grid until it fits the actual budget.
5. Add controlled positive/negative leakage sanity checks, borrowing the base paper's leaky-synthesizer idea. Label these a reproduction/sanity check rather than a new attack.
6. Utility: train a fixed logistic-regression classifier on synthetic data and test on a held-out real set; compare with training on real data. Report macro-F1 and ROC-AUC. Because the bundled control set is finite, reserve a separate utility-test portion before configuring the new experiment and document how this differs from the original full-control audit; alternatively keep the official dataset solely for audit reproduction and create a fresh disjoint train/control/utility-test split from UCI Adult for the entire extension. **Do not fit generator/preprocessing to control or utility-test rows, and do not tune against the final utility test.**

Simplest clean design: original supplied data for Week-2 audit reproduction, then fresh 60/20/20 UCI Adult split for GaussianCopula plus a small locally trained CTGAN if feasible; if CTGAN training is costly, use the supplied CTGAN as one fixed-release case and GaussianCopula as a separate-case comparison and explicitly avoid attributing all differences to generator choice. An even safer core is **audit budget stability on the supplied CTGAN alone**, with two auxiliary-information configurations; generator comparison is an optional extension. Do not muddle these cases when claiming a controlled generator comparison.

### Tools, metrics, outcomes, risks, fallback

Tools: Python, pandas, NumPy, scikit-learn, Anonymeter, SDV/copulas if adding a generator, matplotlib/seaborn. Current Anonymeter advertises Python 3.9–3.13 and has package metadata, source, tests and notebooks. Plain laptop should suffice for the core; memory should be measured at the 2,000-target setting. No GPU is required for auditing a provided synthetic table.

Report per-attack risk and its 95% interval, main/control/naive success rates, valid/ineffective/failed-run counts, elapsed time, variation across audit seeds, and whether rankings are resolved or statistically ambiguous. Plot runtime versus interval width and risk versus budget. Do not average invalid estimates as zeros, select only successful seeds, or call overlapping uncertainty a definitive winner. Paired seeds/targets where supported make comparisons more precise. A high-budget estimate is a reference measurement, not ground-truth privacy risk.

Expected outcomes: small budgets may be noisy, but could also agree with 2,000 targets. Either outcome is useful. If all risks are low, the correct finding is “these attacks did not detect strong excess leakage in these settings,” supported by positive controls, rather than “data are anonymous.” If the proposed generator has worse utility or leakage, publish the trade-off rather than seeking a better accuracy score.

Difficulty **Easy–Moderate**: implementation is mostly dataframes and experiment automation; hardest work is correct control-set design and interpretation. Main risks: weak originality if it is only a notebook rerun; zero/invalid attack findings; long nearest-neighbour searches; API drift; control-set contamination; comparing differently trained releases unfairly. Fallback: one supplied CTGAN release, two fixed auxiliary-knowledge configurations, three budgets, repeated attacks, reported uncertainty and failure rates. No retraining needed. This is sufficient for a rigorous semester report; external publication is not assured.

## Candidate P2 — Regularization and Low-Cost Membership-Inference Auditing of Tabular Classifiers

**Recommendation:** conditional lower-ranked privacy option. Moderate difficulty; greater conceptual and environment risk than P1. Feasible if the team already understands train/validation/test separation, probability outputs and basic PyTorch. Do not deploy against third-party models; train and audit the team's own benchmark models.

### Problem and recent literature

Investigate whether ordinary regularization changes measured privacy risk consistently across a cheap loss-threshold audit and a stronger one-reference-model RMIA audit. Model utility and a simple attack's failure can both look reassuring while another attack detects leakage. This studies privacy of **trained classifiers**, whereas P1 studies privacy of **released synthetic tables**.

### What RMIA does and what was read

RMIA compares the target model's probability for a candidate record with probabilities for reference-population records, normalized using separately trained reference models. It combines pairwise likelihood-ratio evidence into a membership score. Offline mode can work with one OUT reference model; online variants use IN/OUT reference models. Its baselines include loss threshold/Attack-P, Attack-R, LiRA and quantile regression. The paper evaluates CIFAR-10/100, CINIC-10, ImageNet and Purchase-100; neural networks and a GBDT experiment. It reports ROC-AUC and TPR at very low FPR, with ten target models for major comparisons.

For Purchase-100, the paper trains an MLP with layers 512/256/128/64 on 25,000 records for 50 epochs; reported average train/test accuracy is 100%/83.4%. Table 10: with one OUT reference, RMIA AUC is 77.8% ±0.2%, LiRA 59.3% ±0.35%, Attack-R 74.24% ±0.28%; loss-only is 66.62% ±0.27%. **At FPR 0.01%, RMIA TPR is only 0.33% ±0.2%**. High average AUC does not imply a highly effective low-FPR attack. These values are context from the authors, not predictions for a smaller student experiment.

The full paper already studies reference-model counts, reference-population size, architecture/distribution mismatch, GBDT and DP-SGD. Do not advertise any of these alone as a new RMIA contribution. Regularization is also longstanding. Our contribution is a controlled replication/extension measuring **agreement of low-cost audit conclusions across regularization strengths on a bounded tabular setup**, not a novel attack or novel defense.

### Limitations and proposed extension

**Author-stated/setup:** RMIA still uses reference data/models and prediction probabilities, and assumes candidate labels are available for its loss/confidence statistic. The paper's default model/data overlap, calibration and attack game are specified in Appendix A; this is not a label-only attacker or a universal privacy proof. Sections 5.5–5.6 already explore sensitivity, so no claim they ignored it. The paper notes optimal attack performance when target/reference architectures match. No separate exhaustive “limitations” section was found; do not invent an author-stated gap about Adult or imbalance.

**Our inference:** a one-reference audit may rank two regularized classifiers differently from loss thresholding, and low-FPR rankings can be uncertain with a small holdout. This is useful evaluation evidence but modest novelty, especially after the 2026 SoK. A Week-1 precise related-work matrix must establish the combination not already reproduced; if it is, frame it honestly as replication or choose P1/malware instead.

Concrete core: Purchase-100 subset, one MLP architecture, L2 weight decay {0, 1e-4, 1e-2}, three data/training seeds. For each target train one disjoint OUT reference under the same learning setup. Compare LOSS and offline RMIA on the **same target probabilities and candidate set**. Nine targets plus nine references = 18 model fits. Avoid dozens of shadow models, FL, DP accounting, LLMs, image ResNets and parameter tuning on target membership labels. Include one deliberately overfitted positive-control target if ordinary settings show no attack signal. The extra setting is a diagnostic, not a result to selectively substitute into the main grid.

Metrics: classifier macro-F1/accuracy/ROC-AUC; train-test loss gap; attack ROC-AUC, TPR at 1% FPR (optionally 0.1% only with enough nonmembers); expected positive predictive value under declared membership prevalences, if desired; training/audit time and model size. Save raw scores. Use disjoint threshold-calibration and final attack-evaluation samples; preserve actual member/nonmember counts, record uncertainty, and avoid presenting zero observed FPs as zero population FPR. Set the threshold on calibration data rather than selecting the best threshold using the final audit labels. Do not claim DP guarantees for L2 or early stopping.

## P1 final scope refinement and statistical safeguards

For the final recommendation, prefer the unambiguous smallest core: **one fixed supplied CTGAN release, audit-budget stability, two fixed auxiliary-feature configurations, and synthetic leakage sanity checks**. A GaussianCopula comparison is optional; do not make a new generator or downstream utility split a dependency. The academic title can be **How Much Auditing Is Enough? Reproducibility of Attack-Based Privacy Estimates for Synthetic Tabular Data**. The title identifies the actual contribution more precisely than claiming a new privacy–utility technique.

The experiment extension is an explicit reproducibility harness with saved sample manifests and proper repeated-measure analysis:

1. Keep original, synthetic and control tables fixed. For each independent audit replicate, draw an ordered list of original targets and a separate ordered list of control targets. Use nested prefixes of lengths 250/1,000/2,000. Reuse each replicate's targets across auxiliary-feature configurations so the comparisons are paired. Sample without replacement inside a replicate.
2. Do **not** create nested budgets simply by calling `numpy.random.seed` and assuming every evaluator uses the same random generator. Current [inference source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/statice/anonymeter/main/src/anonymeter/evaluators/inference_evaluator.py) samples internally with pandas and does not expose a target-list argument; [singling-out source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/statice/anonymeter/main/src/anonymeter/evaluators/singling_out_evaluator.py) uses NumPy Generator and Polars. Pin the exact revision and add a minimal explicit target/query-manifest adapter to a local fork, or run independent declared audits if that change proves difficult. Save the patch and verify the adapter reproduces original evaluator outputs when given the same targets/guesses. This is a concrete manageable code extension.
3. For **singling out**, budget counts queries/predicates, not ordinary original-record targets. Keep the evaluation tables fixed; save an ordered bank of uniquely generated predicates per replicate and use prefixes, keeping query-generation failures in the record. Never shrink the original/control tables with budget: uniqueness depends on their size. If query-prefix replay complicates control-size correction, restrict the nested-sampling contribution to inference and linkability and keep singling-out as a fixed-budget baseline. This is a sound fallback.
4. A largest-budget audit is not ground truth, and reused prefixes are correlated. Analyze paired differences between budgets across independent replicate manifests; never pool overlapping targets from 250/1,000/2,000 as three independent datasets. A block bootstrap should resample full independent audit replicates, not duplicated rows across budgets. With only five repetitions, emphasize descriptive intervals and plots; do not claim precise nominal-coverage estimates or universal sample-complexity laws.
5. “Confidence interval coverage” is inappropriate against the large-budget estimate. Say **agreement with an independently sampled larger audit** or **interval width/stability**, carrying uncertainty for both. To estimate true coverage, a separate simulation with known population probabilities and many independent trials would be required; this can be an optional synthetic sanity check, not the main claim. Likewise, leaked-record fraction does not automatically equal every attack's detectable risk: compare response curves, not force exact equality outside the base paper's matching settings.
6. Runtime must include target/query generation, main/control/naive attacks and scoring. If scores are cached and prefix-resampled, distinguish this analysis runtime from time needed for a fresh operational audit. Do not claim a speedup from excluding expensive work.

This scope supplies a clear small code contribution, reproducibility experiments and limitations without promising a new attack, a better synthesizer or formal privacy certification. It is a safer eight-week choice than full RMIA or DP-SGD, but the research novelty is deliberately modest.
